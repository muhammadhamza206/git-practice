# Git Practice
Article: "Building a Modern Bank Backend" (https://monzo.com/blog/2016/09/19/building-a-modern-bank-backend)
**What I found interesting in this article:**
I work on SME lending products, so most of what I see day to day is the front half of a financial system: the application flow, the credit decision, the operational handoffs.This article is about the other half which is the infrastructure a bank actually runs on and it's useful corrective, because it shows how many of the constraints I treat as product decisions are really architectural ones settles long before anyone drew a customer journey. 
The part I keep coming back to is how early Monzo committed to a distributed microservices architecture. They had three backend engineers. Conventional startup advice says build a monolith, ship fast, split it up when the pain forces you to. They went the other way, and the reasoning is specific rather than ideological: a bank cannot take a maintenance window, the failure of one component shouldn't take down payments, and teams need to deply independently. Those requirements don't relax as you grow, so they designed for them on day one. What makes the argument honest is that they paired it with pragmatism elsewhere and picked tools that their small team already knewm and said openly that some choices would be revisited. The later migration from Mesos to Kubernetes, which cut infrastructure costs by roughly 78%, is that revision happening in public. 
The second thing worth sitting with is their use of Kafka as a distributed commit log rather than a message queue. A queue delivers a message and forgets it; a commit log keeps an ordered, replayable history. In a lending context that's the difference between knowing a loan was disbursed and being able to reconstruct exactly what the system knew, and when, at the moment the decision was made. Regulators ask that question. So do postmortems. It looks like plumbing and turns out to be a compliance capability. The honest caveat is that the post is from 2016 and parts of it read as period pieces now. But the reasoning holds up better than most architecture writing from that era, mostly because it argues from constraints rather than from trends.

I liked how this article shows that Monzo's big design choices came from real limits, not from following trends. The point about using Kafka as a log that keeps a full history, instead of a queue that forgets messages, stood out to me the most. As a computer science student, I usually think about getting a program to work, but this made me realize that in a bank it matters just as much to be able to go back and see exactly what happened and when. It was also interesting that a team of only three backend engineers chose microservices from the start, since most advice says to start simple and split things up later.
